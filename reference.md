# What good looks like, and the rules worth knowing

Short notes for IES master's students. Rules of thumb, not regulations. Where the faculty,
the course Moodle or your supervisor say something different, they are right and this is
wrong.

---

## The numbers students actually ask about

**One standard page is 1,800 characters including spaces.** Everything below is counted that
way, not in pages as your word processor shows them.

**The thesis.** At least 50 standard pages, 90,000 characters, measured from the introduction
to the conclusion. The course documents are not quite consistent about what is excluded: the
FAQ says abstract, cover pages, proposal and appendices, while the JEM002 page also excludes
references. If you are anywhere near the boundary, count it the strict way (references out)
and ask your seminar teacher rather than relying on either wording. For a thesis in Czech
or Slovak it is 60 pages, 108,000 characters. This is a floor, not a target. Your thesis is
judged on quality, and a padded thesis reads as a padded thesis.

**The first part, in Seminar I.** At least 15 standard pages, 27,000 characters of real text,
with a full reference list. If your thesis is empirical you also show summary statistics —
means and standard errors of your variables — which is how you demonstrate you actually have
the data. The JEM001 page gives the deadline as the end of December for the winter semester or the end
of July for the summer one. The course FAQ says end of June for the summer semester, so the two
disagree; go by the course page or ask your seminar teacher. Either way it is fixed and does
not move.

**The proposal.** Two to three pages, on the template, signed by you and your supervisor.
Three hypotheses that some possible result could contradict, a specific plan for testing each
one, a named data source, a statement of what you expect to add, an outline, and at least five
references.

**Submission dates change every year.** Three windows, roughly January, May and July, but the
exact dates move — the 2026/27 calendar is not the 2025/26 calendar, and getting this wrong
by a day is fatal. Take the date from the current FSV academic calendar and the IES final-exam
page, never from a document from last year, and never from a chatbot.

## How your thesis is judged

Your supervisor and your opponent each write a report on the same four headings:

| Heading | What the reader is asked to judge |
|---|---|
| **Contribution** | Original ideas, critical thinking, a distinct value added |
| **Methods** | Tools relevant to the question and adequate to your level; the topic comprehensively analysed |
| **Literature** | Command of recent literature, properly cited |
| **Manuscript form** | Structure, language, academic presentation of tables and figures, complete bibliography |

Both reports end with questions you will be asked at the defence, and you can read them
before you defend. Answering them well is a large part of how the defence goes.

Worth knowing: **contribution is the heading with the most room in it**. Methods, literature
and form cluster near the top for most theses. Contribution is where a thesis is actually
distinguished, and it is the one thing you cannot fix in the last month. Decide what you are
adding early.

Also worth knowing: **your opponent is the harsher reader**, systematically, not
occasionally. Write for them.

## The stages, and what each one is really for

**An idea.** You have a topic and some reading. The job is to turn it into a question
somebody would want answered, and to find out whether you can get the data. Most theses that
go badly go badly here, and the damage only becomes visible months later.

**A proposal.** The job is to commit: a question, three testable hypotheses, a method that can
test them, and a named data source. Write the contribution sentence now — why the answer here
could differ from what is already known — because if you cannot write it, the project is not
ready, and that is far cheaper to discover now.

**The first part.** Introduction, literature, data, and how you will do it. The point is to
show you can write academic prose and that the data exists. Summary statistics are the proof.

**Results.** You have estimates. The job is to find out whether they survive contact with an
alternative specification, and to be honest with yourself about what would change your
conclusion.

**A full draft.** Everything is present but not yet joined up. The job is coherence: does the
conclusion answer the question the introduction asked, and does the abstract state what you
actually found?

**Nearly done.** The job is the reader's experience, and the defence. Read your own thesis as
an opponent would and write down the four questions you would ask. Those are the questions you
will get.

## The defence

You prepare a short handout. The example the course gives has five parts, and they are a good
structure for thinking about the whole thesis, not just the defence:

1. **Importance** — why this topic matters, in plain terms.
2. **Problem formulation** — your hypotheses, briefly.
3. **Methodology and data** — what you did and why it was the right approach.
4. **Contribution** — what is new. This is the part to get right.
5. **Response to comments from the reports** — careful answers to what your supervisor and
   opponent raised. Do not skip this one: you can read both reports before the defence, and
   answering them well is much of how the defence goes.

At the defence, the committee member assigned to your thesis has read it and both reports
closely; the rest of the committee has the reports in front of them. How you answer the
questions in those reports matters as much as the thesis itself.

## Things that reliably cost students marks

Promising something in the proposal and quietly not doing it. Naming a limitation and then
doing nothing about it — naming it earns very little, attempting it earns a lot. A causal
claim with no design behind it. One specification and no robustness. Tables that cannot be
read without hunting through the text. Pasting raw software output into the thesis. Leaving
template text in the document. A literature review that is a list of summaries with no
argument.

## Reading

Both are in the course materials and both are short enough to actually read.

- Bellemare, *How to Write Applied Papers in Economics* — the structure of an empirical paper.
- McCloskey, *Economical Writing* — how to write a sentence.

And read two or three past IES theses, ideally award-winning ones, with their proposals. They
are published in the university repository with both referee reports attached, so you can see
exactly what the readers praised and what they complained about. That is the single most
useful hour you can spend before you start writing.

## Using AI

Charles University requires you to declare your use of generative AI in your thesis, and using
this tool counts. Declaring is not held against you; not declaring can be. Say which tool you
used and what for, say that you verified the output and that the research is yours, and talk
to your supervisor about it in advance. The university publishes a template and guidance at
`ai.cuni.cz`.

Do not paste a draft anywhere without checking that your supervisor is content and that your
data licence allows it.
